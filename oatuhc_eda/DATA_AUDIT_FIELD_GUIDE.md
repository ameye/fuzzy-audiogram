410 ear records from 205 patients, tested between January 2020 and June 2026

## 1. What this document is, and why it matters

We have checked all 410 ear records in this study before any analysis begins. Most of what we found is small and easy to put right: a date written a different way, a box left empty, a number that looks as if it belongs in the other column. That is ordinary in a busy clinic, and none of it is a criticism of the people who did the work. The point is to catch it now, while the charts are still within reach, so that the study rests on figures everybody has seen and agreed.

A record here means one ear. Each patient has two, so the file holds 410 records from 205 patients.

The guide has four parts. Section 2 is the summary and takes a minute. Section 3 is the list of questions we need answered, and it is the part we would like back first. Section 4 is about the next set of records and how to keep them clean as they are collected. Section 5 takes each problem in the current set in turn and says what to do about it.

## 4. Improving collection from here

Sections 2 and 3 deal with the current file: what is wrong with it, and what we still need to ask. This section is about the next set of records, and over time it is the part that matters most. None of it needs extra staff or extra equipment. Most of it costs seconds per patient, and some of it is probably being done already, at least some of the time. The aim is to do it the same way every time, so that the next check of the file finds less to correct.

### 4.1 Before the patient leaves the room

Of all the checks in this guide, this is the one worth building into the routine.

Read the bone column against the air column at each frequency while the patient is still in the chair. If a bone result is better than the air result by more than 10 dB, stop. That is not a finding about the patient. It means one of the two entries is in the wrong place, or a masked result has gone into the unmasked box.

The timing is what makes it work. With the audiogram in front of you and the patient still present, the test can be repeated, or the entry corrected, in less than a minute. Two years later the same error may be impossible to settle: the chart may be missing, the patient may have moved, and nobody remembers the visit.

It is also the one check a supervisor can make from a few feet away, because it needs no arithmetic, only a comparison of two columns.

### 4.2 The maximum output code

Agree one way to record "no response at the maximum output of the audiometer", and use it every time.

Whatever the mark is, it must not be a number that could be read as a hearing level. A 120 in a threshold box looks exactly like a measured 120, and everything downstream treats it as one. That is how 143 boxes in this file came to sit at the top of the scale.

Two things make the convention stick. Write it down and put it where the work happens: a short note beside the audiometer, saying what the mark is and showing one example. And record the machine's maximum output once at the top of the audiogram, so that a reader can see what the ceiling meant on that day.

If the form is ever redesigned, a separate "no response" box beside each threshold is worth the space. Until then, one agreed mark used by everyone is enough.

### 4.3 Tested but not written down, and not tested

An empty box is ambiguous. It can mean the frequency was not tested, that it was tested and the result was lost, or that nobody wrote it down. Three different situations, one appearance.

Record which one it is. "Not tested" is enough. "Not tested, could not tolerate the earphone" is better, because it tells the next reader something about the patient as well as about the form.

The same applies when a frequency drops out of the routine. If 3 kHz stopped being tested in 2022, or stopped being written down, one note on the form for that period explains a gap that otherwise looks like 200 separate omissions.

The rule underneath all of this is simple: a blank should never be the only record of a decision.

### 4.4 Dates

One format, written down, with an example beside it: Sep 2022. Three letters for the month, a capital first letter, a space, four digits for the year.

If month and year are all that is needed, say so on the form. A form that asks for exactly what it needs gets a consistent answer. A form that asks for "date" without saying which parts matter gets a different habit from every person who fills it in.

### 4.5 Diagnosis

Enter the diagnosis when the notes give one, using the exact wording on the list and the same capitalisation.

When the notes do not give one, say so. "Not documented" is a fact about the notes. An empty box is not: it could mean the notes were silent, or that the notes were not available, and the study cannot tell the difference afterwards.

The same care applies to "other". It is a useful option, but only if the diagnosis is written in the free-text space beside it. Without that, "other" becomes a bin that nothing can be recovered from.

### 4.6 The clinical grade

This one is a rule rather than a habit.

If the consultant's grade is to be used as an independent clinical comparison, it has to be recorded by the clinician who saw the patient, from the notes, at the time of the consultation. It cannot be assigned later from the audiogram by whoever is entering the data, because then it is not an independent opinion. It is the same audiogram read twice, and the comparison proves nothing.

For the records still to be collected, the grade should be written in the clinic, initialled and dated. Where no grade was written, an empty box is the correct entry, and it is a more useful fact than a filled box that nobody can explain.

### 4.7 Exclusions

If an ear cannot be used, mark it when you notice, and say why in one line: poor cooperation, a doubt about the equipment, a repeat test that replaces the earlier one, a chart that cannot be read.

An exclusion recorded during collection is evidence. The same exclusion reconstructed two years later is a guess, and a reviewer will treat it as one. Marking it at the time also protects the rest of the file, because a record set aside with a reason does not have to be defended later.

### 4.8 A short check for the supervisor

Once a week, or once a day where the volume allows, a supervisor can scan a batch of new audiograms for three things. It takes a few minutes and needs no charts.

| Check | What to look for | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Bone against air | A bone number more than 10 higher than the air number at the same frequency | Ask for the test to be repeated, or the entry corrected, before the chart is filed |
| The maximum output mark | A number where the mark should be, or a mark that is not the agreed one | Confirm the entry with the tester and correct it |
| Dates | Any date not in the agreed form, and any blank | Return the sheet to the person who wrote it |

A short note back to the person who did the test is enough. It is a correction, not a report, and the point is to catch the three most common problems in this file on the day they are made rather than a year later.

### 4.9 What this is worth

None of this asks anyone to work more slowly. Most of it is a matter of writing one thing instead of nothing, or using the same mark twice in a row.

The value is in what the next set of records can be used for. A file that is clean as it is collected can be analysed in a week, added to when the clinic wants more ears, and handed to a reviewer without a second round of chart pulls. That is the difference between a study that can be repeated and one that cannot.

## 5. The issues, one at a time

Each section says what we found, how many records, why it matters, and what to do. Study IDs are given so that the chart can be pulled without searching. Study IDs are not patient names, and no patient is named anywhere in this guide.

### 5.1 Bone conduction recorded worse than air conduction

**What we found.** In 59 ears, 14.4% of the file, the bone conduction result is worse than the air conduction result by more than 10 dB at one or more frequency. In 10 of those ears the difference is more than 20 dB, and those cannot be true.

The 59 ears are studies 21, 43, 44, 54, 57, 78, 82, 96, 107, 108, 110, 119, 137, 141, 142, 146, 147, 153, 154, 155, 160, 180, 184, 194, 214, 216, 221, 222, 226, 232, 237, 239, 246, 248, 254, 256, 265, 267, 270, 272, 289, 290, 295, 296, 309, 311, 315, 316, 323, 324, 332, 343, 344, 353, 354, 370, 379, 386, 393.

Most of them, 43 of the 59, have the problem at 2 kHz.

**Why it cannot be true.** Sound heard through bone goes straight to the inner ear. Sound heard through the air has to get past the outer and middle ear first, and then reaches the same inner ear. So for one ear at one frequency, the bone result can never be worse than the air result. It can be equal, and it is often better, which is exactly what a conductive loss looks like. A bone result that is worse means that one of the two numbers belongs somewhere else: the wrong ear, the wrong frequency, or the wrong test.

A little reversal is normal and is allowed. Bone can come out up to 10 dB better than air because of ordinary test-to-test variation, the limits of the bone vibrator, an unmasked bone result picking up the other ear, and the sensation of vibration at high levels. That is why the line is drawn at 10 dB, and why a gap of exactly 10 dB needs no change.

**Likely causes.** The two columns swapped. A masked result and an unmasked result mixed up. A simple typing slip, such as 55 entered as 75.

**What to do**

- Pull the audiogram for each of the 59 records and compare the bone and air entries at the frequency where bone is worse.
- If the chart shows the correct values, correct the record and note what was changed.
- If the chart cannot settle it, mark the ear as unmatched and give the reason, for example "bone and air columns look swapped; chart unclear". Do not guess.
- Start with the 10 ears that are more than 20 dB out: studies 54, 82, 107, 180, 194, 237, 289, 315, 332 and 386. A 20 dB reversal at a speech frequency is not something the ear can do.
- See question 5, on whether masked and unmasked results share a column.

### 5.2 Air-bone gaps larger than 60 dB

**What we found.** 15 ears have bone conduction better than air conduction by more than 60 dB: studies 3, 4, 48, 51, 52, 64, 73, 219, 220, 230, 236, 250, 259, 363 and 364.

**Why it matters.** A gap in this direction is the normal direction for a conductive loss, because bone skips the blocked outer or middle ear. Genuinely large gaps occur in otosclerosis and in chronic suppurative otitis media. But a gap of 70 to 90 dB, with bone sitting between 20 and 50, is more than most conductive losses produce. In this file such gaps are more often a masked result recorded in the unmasked box, or two different tests joined into one row. If the gap is real it changes the diagnosis. If it is not real, it changes the grade.

**What to do**

- Pull each chart and read the air and bone columns together.
- Where the gap is real, leave it and note in the record which diagnosis explains it.
- Where the chart shows a masked result, enter the masked result in the masked box.
- Do not adjust either number by arithmetic. If the chart does not show it, the study lead needs to know rather than receive a plausible guess.

### 5.3 The value 120

**What we found.** 143 boxes in the file contain exactly 120. That is 4.7% of the 3,065 threshold boxes, and they sit in 54 ears, 13.2% of the file.

| Frequency | 250 Hz | 500 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz | 3 kHz | 4 kHz | 6 kHz | 8 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boxes at 120 | 2 | 9 | 13 | 19 | 12 | 23 | 40 | 25 |

**Why it is a problem.** A threshold is the quietest level at which the patient responded. 120 is not that. It is what gets written when the patient does not respond at the loudest level the audiometer can produce, and the true threshold is unknown and somewhere at or beyond that limit. Written as an ordinary number it makes the hearing look better than it is, because the loss is at least as deep as 120 and may be deeper. The study then reads it as if it were a measurement.

Three things in the file show that this is what happened.

- **25 boxes at 8 kHz.** Most audiometers cannot produce 120 at 8 kHz; the top of the range there is usually between 90 and 110. A 120 at 8 kHz is the recorder writing down the machine's maximum, not a level the patient heard.
- **27 ears have 120 at 6 kHz and a better result at 8 kHz.** A patient cannot fail to hear 120 at 6 kHz and then hear 100 at 8 kHz. The 27 ears are studies 1, 2, 55, 56, 64, 67, 83, 84, 93, 94, 121, 129, 131, 132, 138, 176, 207, 220, 240, 257, 287, 292, 298, 305, 306, 357 and 364. Sixteen of them also have 120 at 4 kHz.
- **21 ears carry both a 120 and a 115**, and 115 is the highest value anywhere in the file below 120. Nothing above 120 exists, so 120 is the end of the scale rather than a reading on it.

**How much it matters.** Because 120 sits at the very top of the scale, the figure that decides the grade moves by at most 1 dB whether 120 is treated as a number or as "no response", and no ear changes grade. The grade is therefore not at risk. What is at risk is the shape of the audiogram at 6 and 8 kHz, which the study uses, and any sentence about how deep a loss is. 28 ears carry a 120 at one of the four frequencies that decide the grade, and 4 ears (studies 55, 56, 83 and 84) are 120 at all four.

**What to do**

- Do not change any 120 to another number. It is not a typing error.
- Confirm with the audiology supervisor and the study lead that 120 on the form means "no response at the maximum the audiometer can produce", and whether that maximum is the same at every frequency.
- From now on record it one way. If the form has a "no response" or "maximum output" box, tick it. If it has not, agree a single mark such as NR, write the mark in the box, and record the machine's limit once at the top of the audiogram.
- For records already entered, leave the 120 and tell the study lead which ears they are. The study will handle them together rather than ear by ear.
- See questions 2 and 3.

### 5.4 3 kHz not recorded

**What we found.** 200 ears, 48.8% of the file, have no 3 kHz result. The gap is not spread evenly; it starts in the middle of 2022 and is complete from 2024.

| Year | Ears | With no 3 kHz | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 92 | 2 | 2% |
| 2021 | 88 | 0 | 0% |
| 2022 | 84 | 56 | 67% |
| 2023 | 0 | no records | not applicable |
| 2024 | 94 | 94 | 100% |
| 2025 | 42 | 40 | 95% |
| 2026 | 8 | 8 | 100% |

It splits evenly between the ears, 100 left and 100 right, so it is not one examiner or one side.

**Why it matters.** 3 kHz is where a noise notch shows, and the study reads the shape of the audiogram as well as its level. Without 3 kHz, a notch cannot be seen in the usual way. Because the change begins in the middle of 2022, records from 2024 onward cannot be compared with records from 2020 and 2021 at that frequency. The study can fill the gap by estimating from 2 and 4 kHz, but an estimate is not a measurement, and the study has to say so wherever it uses one.

The question for the team is simple. Was 3 kHz tested and not written down, or not tested at all? The two need different answers, and one answer covers the whole period.

**What to do**

- Check a sample of charts from 2024 and 2025. If 3 kHz was tested, add it. If it was not tested, write "not tested" in the box rather than leaving it empty.
- Give the study lead one answer for the whole period, not ear by ear.
- See question 4.

### 5.5 Dates in different formats

**What we found.** 58 records, 14.1% of the file, carry a test date that is not in the standard form. There are 50 different spellings across 39 months of testing.

### 5.6 The diagnosis field

**What we found.** 181 ears, 44.1%, carry a named diagnosis. Another 137 (33.4%) are recorded as "other" and 92 (22.4%) as "not documented". That leaves 229 ears, 55.9%, without a named diagnosis. Fifteen records spell chronic suppurative otitis media as "csoM" instead of "csom".

| Diagnosis | Ears | Diagnosis | Ears |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presbyacusis | 76 | Meniere | 32 |
| Ototoxicity | 24 | Otosclerosis | 20 |
| Chronic suppurative otitis media | 15 | Noise-induced loss | 10 |
| Sudden sensorineural loss | 4 | **All named diagnoses** | **181** |

**Why it matters.** A blank is usable. It says honestly that the notes named no diagnosis, and the study can treat those ears as unknown and say so. An inconsistent entry is not usable, because one diagnosis ends up in two places. "csoM" and "csom" are the same disease, but as written the 15 ears fall outside the chronic suppurative otitis media group, and a count of that group comes out 15 short without anyone seeing why.

"Other" also needs care. It is the largest single group in the file, but it is a mixture of different conditions rather than a diagnosis, so a result quoted for "other" means very little.

**What to do**

- Use the exact wording on the list, with the same capitalisation.
- Where the notes name a diagnosis, enter it.
- Where the notes name something that is not on the list, choose "other" and write the diagnosis in the free-text space so it is not lost.
- Where the notes say nothing, choose "not documented". Do not infer a diagnosis from the audiogram.
- Change the 15 "csoM" entries to "csom".
- See question 7.

### 5.7 The grade recorded by the consultant

**What we found.** The consultant's grade box is filled for all 410 ears: 58 normal, 111 mild, 59 moderate, 94 moderately severe, 39 severe and 49 profound. There is not one blank.

The protocol says this box is filled only where the consultant's opinion is written in the notes, and left blank otherwise. A box that is never blank, in notes reviewed years after the event, is not what that rule leads you to expect.

**Why it matters.** This grade is the human opinion that the study compares its own grading against. If it was copied from the consultant's note, it is an independent opinion and the comparison tells us something worth knowing. If it was decided when the record was entered, by looking at the same audiogram the study already uses, then it is a second copy of the same numbers and the comparison proves nothing. The study cannot present it as a clinical comparison in that case, and it has to say which it is.

**What to do**

- Answer question 1 for the file as a whole, and list the ears, if any, where the grade was assigned rather than found.
- Do not change any of the grades.
- A partial answer is more useful than a tidy one. "All copied" and "all assigned" are both answers the study can work with; "probably copied" is not.

### 5.8 Ears excluded

**What we found.** No ear in the file is marked as excluded. All 410 are marked as included, and no exclusion reason is recorded for any of them.

**Why it matters.** A record can be unusable for reasons that have nothing to do with the hearing result: a chart that cannot be read, a test that was abandoned halfway, an ear that turns out to belong to a different patient. The protocol allows those to be set aside, but only if they are marked. At present the file says that every ear is usable, including the 10 with a bone result that cannot be true and the 15 with a gap of more than 60 dB.

**What to do**

- The decision belongs to the study lead, not to the person entering data. What is needed from the field is a list: which records have a reason to be set aside, and what the reason is.
- Mark those ears as not included and add a one-line reason. Leave the thresholds as they are; the record is set aside, not edited.
- See question 6.

### 5.9 Two patients with the same result in both ears

**What we found.** Two patients have exactly the same air conduction result in the left ear and the right ear: patient 28, studies 55 and 56, and patient 42, studies 83 and 84. Both patients are at or near 120 at every frequency.

**Why it matters.** Perfectly symmetrical profound loss is possible, and these may be genuine. A record copied from one ear into the other looks exactly the same, and the difference matters, because a copied record counts as two ears when it is one. These four ears are also four of the ears that are 120 at all four speech frequencies.

**What to do**

- Pull both charts and confirm that the two audiograms were done separately, on the same day or on different days.
- If they were, leave the records and note that they were confirmed.
- If one is a copy, mark the second ear as not included and say so.

### 5.10 The gaps in collection

**What we found.** There are no records at all from 2023, in a file that runs from January 2020 to June 2026. Across the whole period, only 40 of the 78 months carry a record. The months that do carry records come in bursts: 11 separate runs, from 2 ears to 94. Five months hold exactly 22 ears, and no month holds more than 32.

| Year | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ears | 92 | 88 | 84 | **0** | 94 | 42 | 8 |

**Why it matters.** This is not a mistake in one record. It decides whether the 410 ears stand for the clinic's patients or only for the months that happened to be pulled. A missing year cannot be made up by adding ears from other years, and five months holding exactly 22 ears looks more like a target than like a list of everyone who was tested.

**What to do**

- Answer from what you know of the department. Were audiograms done at OAUTHC in 2023? If they were, where are those records now: which book, which shelf, which office?
- Do the months with records match the months the clinic was busiest, or were records pulled in batches to reach a number?
- Pass both answers to the study lead. Neither is a criticism. A lost year and a batch extraction are both ordinary, and the study simply has to say which it was.

## 6. What is already in good shape

A guide that lists only faults teaches people to stop reporting them, so this section matters as much as the one before it. Most of this file is sound.

- **Every patient has both ears.** 205 patients, 410 records, 205 left and 205 right. No study number appears twice, and no patient has two different ages.
- **All six grades are well represented.** 95 normal, 65 mild, 70 moderate, 79 moderately severe, 54 severe and 47 profound. No grade holds fewer than 47 ears, so the study can compare every band properly.
- **Ages run from 18 to 98**, with the middle patient at 60. Nothing falls outside the range the protocol allows.
- **The sample is unusually rich in borderline cases.** 236 ears, 57.6%, sit within 5 dB of a grade boundary. That is exactly what this study needs, because it tests the grading where grading is hard rather than where it is easy.
- **The thresholds themselves are almost complete.** Every ear has a result at 250 Hz, 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 4 kHz and 8 kHz on air conduction. 3 kHz is the exception described above, and 6 kHz is missing for only 15 ears. On bone, only 9 or 10 ears are missing a value at any of the four speech frequencies.
- **Sex is recorded for every patient**, 158 male ears and 252 female.

None of this needs any action. It is here so that the team can see what the file gets right, which is most of it.

## 7. How to avoid this next time

The whole of section 4 in six lines. Print this page and keep it where the audiograms are written.

1. **Write the date the same way every time.** Three letters, capital first letter, four-digit year. Sep 2022. Never leave it blank.
2. **Never leave a threshold box empty without saying why.** Write "not tested" if it was not tested, and the agreed mark if the patient did not respond. A blank box tells the next reader nothing, and it hides a decision that was made at the time.
3. **Look down the bone column before you close the audiogram.** If a bone number is larger than the air number at the same frequency by more than 10, stop and check both entries. It takes ten seconds and it catches most of section 5.1.
4. **Use the maximum-output mark the same way every time.** 120 is the top of the audiometer's range, not a hearing level. Do not write it as an ordinary threshold, and do not write a different mark on different days.
5. **Complete the diagnosis box from the notes.** Exact wording from the list, "other" only when a diagnosis is named but not listed, "not documented" when the notes are silent.
6. **Mark the ear you cannot use, with a reason.** One line is enough. A record set aside with a reason can be explained to a reviewer; a record that quietly stays in cannot.

## 8. Who to ask

Questions about any record go to the study lead. Quote the study ID and the frequency and say what the chart shows, and the answer will come back the same week.

If you are unsure whether a value is right, do not change it. Flag it and ask. A flagged record can be fixed and the fix can be checked. A silent correction cannot be checked by anybody, including the person who made it.

Thank you for the work behind these 410 records. Getting them checked now is what makes the study's result stand up later.
